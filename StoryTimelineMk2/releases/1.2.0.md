## Characters

- **Characters have a window of their own.** The Characters button in the side strip opens a list
  of everyone in the timeline, with a form for each: first and last name, nicknames, aliases,
  race, faction, a state (alive, dead, missing — or anything you type), importance, color,
  description and notes.
- **Faction.** Whatever your world divides itself into — a house, a guild, an army, a cult. It is
  free text, and the box suggests the names you have already used so a cast does not end up split
  between *Night Watch* and *night watch*. The relations views group people by it, and it is
  searchable in the character list.
- **Portraits.** Give a character a picture and their face shows up beside their name in the
  list. Replacing it clears out the old one.
- **Birth and death dates follow your calendar.** They are entered exactly the way item dates
  are, at whatever level of detail you need — a year, a month, a day.
- **Put a character on the timeline.** Tick *Show on timeline* and their birth and death appear
  as events in the character's own color, tagged and attached to them, carrying their portrait
  as a picture. Move a date and the events move with it; untick the box and they go away.

- **Characters name themselves into your items.** Type a character's name — or a nickname, or an
  alias — into an item's description or content and it lights up as you write. When you click
  away they are attached to the item, marked as something the app found rather than something you
  added. Take one off and it stays off.
- **Invent a character without leaving the item.** The character picker in the item editor has a
  *+ New* button that makes one from whatever you typed into the filter box.
- **Every item a character is in, on their own page.** The character form lists their appearances
  with the role you gave them; click one to fly the timeline to it, or the pencil to open it.
- **Birth and death items look like the character.** They draw as a round portrait on the
  timeline instead of a box, they fall back to the character's color when there is no portrait,
  and they can be filtered as *Character*.
- **Edit the character from their birth or death.** Right-click one of those events and pick *Edit
  character*, or open it and use the button next to *Edit item* — either opens the Characters
  window on that character. *Edit item* still edits the event itself.
- **The Characters window opens without the wait.** It is made ready in the background while you
  work, and made ready again each time you close it.
- **Portraits are no longer drowned in color.** The disc behind a portrait is neutral now, with
  the character's color on the ring around it and the stem below — so a picture with a
  transparent background reads as a face instead of a silhouette. Tick *Use highlight color* on a
  character if you liked the filled disc; it now thickens the ring as well, so it shows on a
  portrait that covers the disc completely.
- **Long captions fold onto a second line.** A character's name or a picture's caption that does
  not fit across the image now wraps to two lines instead of losing half of itself.
- **Nothing is drawn through a character's face.** A portrait is several rows tall, and the
  timeline now packs events around all of them instead of only the row the portrait starts in.
- **Highlighted names have room to breathe.** The highlight behind a name you typed now has a
  little padding and a soft border, instead of hugging the letters.
- **A timeline of one character.** *Their timeline* — on the *Appears in* header of the character
  form, or on a right-clicked portrait — opens the timeline again with everything that is not
  about that character left out: their appearances, their birth and death, and nothing else. It
  opens read-only, so it is a view of their life, not another place to edit it.
- **Hand over one character's journey.** Export from inside that window and you get a timeline
  file containing only that character — their events, their pictures, their tags — so you can
  give someone a character without giving them your whole world.
- **A line through their life.** In a character's own timeline, a wave in their color runs along
  the middle of the canvas from their birth to their death, so you can see at a glance how much of
  the world's history they were there for. If you have not recorded one of the two dates, the wave
  is drawn dashed and simply carries on off that edge. Ages are drawn a little lighter in that
  window, so the line stays readable where it passes under them.
- **Who they are to everybody else.** The character form has a *Relations* section: tie a
  character to another as parent, sibling, grandparent, cousin, spouse, in-law, friend, colleague,
  rival — twenty kinds in all, family and social — and the relation shows up on both of their
  pages, read the right way round from each — *parent of* on one, *child of* on the other.
  Relations normally need no dates, since a son is one from birth; tick *From* or *Until* for the
  ones that do, like an adoption or a marriage that ended.
- **Relating somebody is a window now.** *Relate* opens a list of everyone else in the timeline
  with their portrait, years and state, and a search box over it — so you pick a face rather than
  a name from a dropdown. The kind you choose is spelled out underneath both ways round (“Mira is
  daughter of Aldric”), and a button swaps the two if you got them backwards. Every relation the
  character already has is listed beside it; click one to change its kind, its dates or its note,
  or to remove it.
- **“Five characters share this name — family?”** When somebody shares a last name with characters
  you have not related them to, the Relations section offers to sort it out. It opens a list of
  every pair, with a guess at each one from their birth years — sixteen years or more apart reads
  as parent and child, closer than that as siblings. The guesses are only the dates talking, so
  every row can be re-kinded, swapped or unticked, and pairs that are already related say so and
  stay out of it. Nothing is written until you press the button.
- **Mother of, not parent of.** Characters have a *Gender* field — a list of suggestions you can
  type past — and relations use it to pick the natural English word: *mother of*, *son of*,
  *aunt of*, *brother-in-law of*. Where English has no gendered word, as with cousins, or where
  the gender is one it has no word for, the relation reads the neutral way, which is the wording
  working rather than a gap in it. You can set the wording yourself for any kind in the *Kinds*
  editor.
- **Their family is on their timeline.** A character's own timeline now also shows the births and
  deaths of everyone they are related to, so a life reads against the people in it. Relations,
  not surnames — an in-law who shares no name is family, a stranger who happens to share one is
  not. Their relatives draw smaller and a little dimmer than the character whose window it is, so
  it stays clear whose life you are looking at.
- **A character's own timeline is ringed in their color**, all the way round the window, so it is
  never mistaken for the timeline itself.
- **Ages and periods can run off the edge.** *Open start* and *open end* on an age or a period draw
  a fading arrow instead of a hard edge — for the war that was already old when the story begins,
  or the dynasty that outlasts it, without stretching the timeline to a year you do not mean. Tick
  either, or both.
- **The characters window opens straight away.** It was being warmed only halfway, so every open
  paid for loading the page in front of you. It now loads in the background while you work and the
  two things it fetches come at once instead of one after the other.
- **Your own kinds of relation.** *Kinds* next to the *Relate* button opens a small editor: add
  “Sworn enemies” or “Liege / sworn”, say how it reads each way, and it is offered everywhere.
  Removing a kind leaves the relations that used it in place.
- **Picture and portrait captions have their own text size.** Titles like “The birth of …” ran out
  of room under a portrait. *Settings → Pictures & Portraits* now has a size for picture captions
  and a separate one for portrait captions, so you can shrink the one without touching the other.
  Existing timelines keep the size they had.

- **Everyone, all at once.** A new *Relations* button in the side strip opens a window with the
  whole cast as a web: every character a circle with their portrait in it, every relation a line
  in the colour of its kind. Drag someone and they stay where you put them, next time too. Click
  a character and everyone more than one step away fades, so you can see their corner of it.
  Double-click to open them in the Characters window; right-click for their own timeline.
  The web shuffles itself into shape for a second or two when it opens and then holds still, so
  you can read it — however big the cast is.
- **The web as it was in a given year.** Tick *As of year* and drag the slider: relations that had
  not started or had already ended disappear, characters who were not born yet fade almost away,
  and the ones who had died are half-lit. Your world in 1204, and then in 1230.
- **"How are they related?"** Pick two characters and the window spells the chain out — *Risha is
  the mother of Adan, who is the wife of Toma* — and traces it through the web. If nothing
  connects them, it says so. It answers from the relations you have recorded, as of the year on the
  scrubber: unticking a kind under *Kinds* changes what the picture draws, not who is related to
  whom. Before, an unticked kind left over from another view could report a married couple drawn
  side by side with their children as strangers — and blank the *Chain* view with them.
- **A family tree.** The other half of the same window: the character you chose in the middle,
  their ancestors above, their descendants below, one row per generation, with couples joined by
  a connector their children hang off — so a child with two parents does not tangle the lines.
  Click any relative to re-centre the tree on them. It is built from parents and marriages only,
  since a grandparent on its own does not say who came between.
- **The family tree is a genogram now.** It used to throw away everything that was not a parent or
  a marriage — two cousins who cannot stand each other looked exactly like two cousins who never
  met. Every other tie between two people on the chart is now drawn over it as a curve in its own
  colour, thicker the closer they are, dashed if it is secret or estranged, and jagged if it is
  hostile. Ties reaching right across a big family are drawn faint, so the near ones — the ones a
  tree is read for — stay on top. Men are squares, women circles, anyone else a diamond, with the
  portrait still inside; a cross through the shape means they are dead. And the tree sizes itself
  to the window when it opens instead of arriving at whatever zoom you left the last view at — a
  family too big to show whole at a readable size opens on the character you picked, with the rest
  a drag away.
- **The year and the kinds now reach the family tree.** *As of year* and the *Kinds* checkboxes
  used to disappear when you opened the tree. They are there now: untick *affinity* to read the
  bare family under the curves, or drag the year to see who was already dead in 1204. Unticking a
  kind only takes those curves away — the tree itself is always drawn, or there would be nothing
  left to look at.
- **Everyone on one line, by when they were born.** The new *Arc* view lays the whole cast along a
  single axis in birth order, oldest on the left, with every relation drawn as a curve — family
  arching over the line, everything else looping under it. Click someone and their own ties light
  up while the rest fade back, which is how you follow one person across a cast too wide to take
  in at once. Anyone you never gave a birth year to waits past a dashed fence at the end rather
  than being dropped or quietly invented.
- **The arc can stack into rows by generation.** A checkbox under the spread slider: everyone
  whose parents are not in your cast goes on the top row, their children on the next one down, and
  so on, with birth order still running left to right. It turns the single line into a picture of
  who came from whom without losing the dates — and couples stay on one row even when one of them
  married in from nowhere.
- **A spread slider for the arc.** All the way left and everyone sits the same distance apart:
  neat, and it tells you only who came before whom. All the way right and the spacing is true to
  the years, so a quiet century is as wide on screen as it was in the story and three generations
  crammed into a decade look crammed. Wherever you leave it is where it is next time you open that
  timeline.
- **Factions, in boxes, with the ties that cross between them.** The new *Sociogram* view gives
  every faction a box of its own and stands them in a ring. Relations that stay inside a box fade
  into the background; the ones that cross from one faction to another are drawn full strength, so
  what you see at a glance is where the loyalties run across the lines you drew. Anyone you never
  gave a faction to rings the outside, belonging to nothing — and since they share a box with
  nobody, every one of their relations counts as a crossing. Click a character and their own
  threads brighten without the rest of the picture going flat. If your houses all deal with each
  other the middle of the ring fills in solid; the **Crossing ties** slider turns the crossings
  down until you can see the boxes again, and remembers where you left it.
- **The *Knots* view actually shows the knots.** The groups were there in the maths, but they
  settled on top of one another, so you could not see them. Now each group of people who mostly
  know each other shoves the others away and sits under a soft coloured blob of its own, labelled
  with whoever in it has the most ties — *Bran Grimsby and 25 others*. The whole picture keeps
  itself sized to the window the whole time it is shuffling, so nothing is ever off the edge. A **Knot distance**
  slider in the sidebar decides how hard the groups push each other away — slide it right for
  clear daylight between them, at the cost of a smaller picture, since the view zooms out to keep
  everything on screen. It reaches twice as far right as it first did, which is what a big cast
  needs, and where you leave it is where it is next time you open that timeline. The knots
  themselves stay as tight as they were wherever you put the slider — it is the threads running
  *between* the groups that give, so pushing the groups apart no longer pulls them to bits.
- **Follow the route across the grid.** When the finder has worked out how two people are
  connected, tick *Show the route on the grid* in the *Matrix* view and it draws the answer:
  every step of the chain is ringed on the square where those two people meet and numbered in
  order, with a dotted line running from one step to the next through the square of whoever they
  have in common. Handy on a big cast, where reading the sentence and then hunting for the squares
  is the slow way round.
- **And along the family chart.** The same tick box is there in the *Genogram* view, where it
  numbers everyone on the route in order and lights the chart's own lines between them — down into
  a marriage, along a row of brothers and sisters — so you can see the answer as a path through
  the family rather than read it as a sentence. It only offers this when both ends are on the
  chart in front of you: a genogram is one family around one root, not the whole cast, and a
  trail that jumped over people who are not drawn would be worse than no trail.
- **A right-click on the family chart stops moving it.** Right-clicking somebody in the *Genogram*
  used to re-centre the whole chart on them at the same moment it opened their menu, sliding the
  person you were aiming at out from under the cursor. Now it rings them and opens the menu, and
  nothing moves; a left click still re-centres, as it always did.
- **Which houses actually deal with each other.** The new *Chord* view stands your factions round
  a circle and runs a ribbon between every pair that has ties, as thick as the number of them. A
  faction's slice is as wide as its dealings, not its size, so a house of forty who keep to
  themselves takes up less of the circle than a house of five everybody needs something from — and
  a ribbon that loops back onto its own slice is a faction that mostly talks to itself. Click any
  slice or ribbon and it lights up while the rest fades, with the list of exactly who it is made
  of down the side; click it again to see the whole picture back. There is a second grouping too:
  switch to *Kinds* and the circle is your relation categories instead, showing how many people
  have family ties and rivalries both.
- **Who nobody knows.** A corner of the sidebar lists the characters no relation mentions at all,
  which is usually the ones you meant to get back to.
- **The web no longer flies off the screen first.** Opening *Knots* used to throw the cast out
  past the edges of the window for a couple of seconds and then snap the whole thing back into
  view once it stopped moving. It now stays framed from the first moment, pulling back as the
  groups spread out and closing in gently as they settle. Grab anyone and it stops following, so
  the picture never moves under your hand.
- **The Relations sidebar and the Characters list can be dragged wider.** Grab the edge beside
  either one and pull: long names stop being cut off, and the width is remembered.
- **Find a face.** A search box over the whole cast pulls whoever you type to the middle of the
  window.
- **A character can belong to every timeline.** Tick *Shared character* and they show up in every
  timeline's cast, while still counting as one person — the same portrait, the same dates, the
  same relations, edited in one place. They keep the timeline they were made in as their home.
- **Say how close two people are.** Every relation now carries a closeness from 0 to 100, and the
  line between them in the Relations window is drawn thicker the closer they are — and pulls them
  harder together when the web lays itself out. New relations start in the middle.
- **Half-sisters, step-fathers, cousins once removed.** A relation can take a degree, which reads
  into its wording everywhere the relation is named: *half-sister of*, *cousin once removed of*.
- **Estranged, secret, adoptive, former, alleged.** A relation can also carry a state word. It
  leads the wording — *estranged half-sister of* — and changes the line: a secret or alleged tie
  is drawn as long dashes, an estranged or former one as short ones.
- **Character and relation dates are as fine as item dates.** Birth, death and the years a
  relation held are now stored the way items are, so a date picked to the day stays on the day
  instead of rounding to its year. Existing characters and relations were converted in place.
- **Matrix.** The whole cast down the side and across the top, with a square wherever two of them
  are related — brighter the closer they are. People who share a faction sit together, the biggest
  house first and everyone who belongs to nothing at the end, so the blocks fall along the
  diagonal; the character you have selected gets their row and column lit up. Click a square and
  the two people it crosses drop into the *How are they related?* boxes, which then spells their
  connection out and traces it into every other view — and the square you picked is outlined,
  with its row and column lit, so you can see what you just asked about. Click a name down the
  side to set the first of the pair, one across the top to set the second.
- **The chain between two people, with what hangs off it.** The new *Chain* view takes the two
  characters in *How are they related?* and lays the route between them out left to right, with
  each step written over its line — *mother of*, *husband of*, *rival of*. Round everybody on the
  way is a small circle for each of the other people they are directly related to, marked with
  their initials: up to five of them, with a *+3* where there are more. So you see not only how
  two characters connect, but who else is standing next to the connection. Drag anyone to tidy the
  picture up — the side circles follow the person they belong to — and untick *Side circles* for
  the bare route. A long route runs off both edges of the window, so there is a **Fit the chain on
  screen** button: it folds the route into rows that read back and forth like lines of writing,
  sized to the window, and zooms out to show all of it. Press it again to straighten the chain
  back out.
- **Right-click means the app now.** Anywhere in the Relations window you get its own menu instead
  of the browser's: on a character, *Their timeline*, *Open in characters*, *Centre the genogram
  here*, and — in every view — the two ends of *How are they related?*, so you can pin down
  somebody you have just spotted and might not find again. On the background, *Fit to window* and
  *Unpin all*. Copy and Save moved to two small buttons in the top-right corner of the picture.
- **Copy and Save give you the whole picture.** They used to hand over the window's worth of it you
  happened to be looking at — no use at all for a family tree or a chain that runs several screens
  wide. Both now write out everything drawn, with a margin round it. A third button beside them
  opens the settings the two share: **what goes behind the picture** — plain, ruled paper, dotted
  paper, or transparent, for dropping it onto a page of your own — and **how large**, at 1×, 2× or
  4×, with the pixel size it will come to shown underneath. A picture too big to encode is scaled
  down to fit rather than saved blank, and the panel says so before you press anything.
- **You do not lose who you clicked.** The selected character is ringed in white in every view, and
  if a re-drawn chart would leave them off the edge the window brings them back into the middle —
  only when they have actually gone out of sight, so a chart that already shows them holds still.
- **The genogram opens as a family tree.** Its three *Kinds* boxes now start unticked, so you get
  the bare tree and add the friendships, rivalries and affinities over it when you want them,
  rather than clearing a thicket before you can read anything.
- **The character window's buttons are along the bottom.** Save and Delete on the left, *Relate* and
  *Their timeline* on the right, in a bar along the bottom of the character's own form — so they are
  in the same place whether you are at the top of it or scrolled to the end. Delete is red standing,
  rather than only under the mouse.
- **The character list can be dragged narrow without the New button escaping it.** It used to slide
  out over the drag handle and onto the form beside it; the search box now gives way instead.
- **Deleting a character asks in the app's own dialog.** It used the browser's grey box, which looks
  nothing like the rest of the app. Removing one of your own kinds of relation now asks the same
  way, and tells you how many relations it will leave showing a bare name.
- **The Relate window stays open after you add a relation.** It used to shut itself the moment a
  relation was written, so tying somebody to three people meant opening it three times. It now just
  adds the new tie to the list down the side and waits for the next one.
- **A family tree in a window of its own.** *Family tree* opens one character's genogram by itself,
  with no sidebar and no other views, small enough to keep beside the Relations window. Click a
  relative to re-centre it on them. It is on the Archive's Characters tab, at the bottom of the
  Characters window, and on a portrait's right-click menu on the timeline. Asking for another
  character's tree while one is open re-centres the open one instead of opening a second.
- **Birthdays on the year calendar.** The year calendar has *Birthdays* and *Deaths* buttons next to
  *Items*. Whatever year you are looking at, everyone alive in it has a small cake on their
  birthday, in their colour, and hovering the day says *Mira turns 30*. Anyone who has died gets a
  cross on the day of their death every year after it: *Aldric died 25 years ago*. Only dates
  picked down to the day show, since a birth known only to the month has no day to put the cake
  on. More than three on one day become a number, and the day's tooltip lists them all.
- **The calendar's day card stays in the window.** Hovering a day near the edge of the year
  calendar used to push its list of events off the side or under the toolbar. It now opens beside
  the pointer and moves to whichever side has room, and a long title wraps instead of running off.

## Maps

- **Your world has a map now.** The map pin in the side strip opens a window of its own: make a map,
  give it a picture, and pin the places on it. Scroll to zoom, drag to pan, and *Fit* puts the whole
  thing back in view.
- **Maps nest as deep as you like.** A pin can open into a map of its own — the world into a kingdom,
  the kingdom into a city, the city into a single house. A pin with a map behind it wears a dashed
  ring; double-click it to go in, and the trail along the top brings you back out. *Give it a map*
  makes the next one down in one click.
- **The list down the side is your places.** Every place you have pinned is in it, whether or not it
  has a map of its own, each one under the place it sits inside — so *Ashvale › The Bent Nail* is one
  look rather than five clicks, and a twisty folds a branch away. Clicking a place takes you to the map
  it is pinned to and puts it in the middle of the view. A place that opens into a map of its own shows
  what is inside it instead of repeating its name, and hovering it gives you a door button that goes
  down into it, next to a pencil that edits whatever the row is.
- **Search finds places, and keeps them in place.** Type in the box above the list: it keeps the list's
  shape and simply hides everything that does not lead to a match, so *Overworld › Ashvale › The Bent
  Nail* stays whole and the Bent Nail in the next city over is never mistaken for it.
- **The map you are on has a card of its own.** It floats over the map rather than sitting in the
  sidebar under the selected place, where it was easy to read as belonging to that marker. It carries
  the map's name, its description and how many places are pinned on it, and an *Edit map* button that
  opens a window for the name, the description, the picture and deleting it — with a Cancel that really
  cancels. Drag the card's title bar to move it, close it with its X, and the ⓘ in the bottom-right
  corner brings it back; where you left it and whether it was up are remembered. The trail along the
  top now names the map you are on even when it is the only one, instead of saying "top level".
- **Pins hold their ground.** A place is stored as a position on the picture rather than in pixels, so
  you can redraw your map, export it at a different size and re-attach it without moving a single pin.
- **Nothing on the map moves by accident.** Moving a place, drawing its name, setting its footprint and
  placing the compass each start from a button in the window that edits it — the window saves what you
  typed, gets out of the way, and comes back when you press *Finish*. Until then everything else on the
  map is fixed, so panning about can no longer drag a village into the sea and save it there.
- **You can see which place you picked.** Clicking a pin sends a ring out from it and leaves a white circle
  round it, so a selected place is obvious on a crowded map instead of being a name in the sidebar.
- **Looking at a place is not editing it.** Click a pin and the panel simply shows it: its name, its
  description, what it opens into, and what happened there — every event you have pointed at that
  place, earliest first. *Edit* opens a window for the name, description and colour, with Cancel that
  really cancels. A brand-new pin opens it straight away so you can name it.
- **Your pins do not have to be dots.** *Edit map* → **Marker** picks what every place on that map
  looks like: a dot, a ring, a teardrop, a badge, a thumbtack or a flag, at whatever size, with your own
  colour and outline — or no outline at all. The ring and the badge hold an icon, three dozen to choose
  from, so a castle, a tavern and a harbour can be told apart at a glance.
- **And one place can break the rule.** *Edit* on a pin has the same Marker panel, and whatever you
  change there applies to that place alone. Everything you leave alone keeps following the map: restyle
  the map later and every ordinary pin moves with it, while the capital you made a flag stays a flag.
  *Back to this map's look* undoes a place's own styling in one click.
- **Put the name where it reads.** The name has a section of its own — **Label**, next to **Marker** —
  because where a name sits has nothing to do with what shape the pin is. It can go to the right, the
  left, above or below its marker, or be hidden altogether, and it can be tilted to any angle, so a name
  can run along a coast or a road. Its size and colour are yours, *card behind it* puts a dark plate
  under the text, and **text outline** draws an edge around the letters themselves — white text in a
  black line reads on a map of any colour, which no plate has to cover up. The outline has a thickness of
  its own if you want one; left at 0 it follows the size of the text.
- **Or place the name by hand, on the map.** *Edit* a place → *Set label design*, and the window steps out
  of the way: the name stands on a line with a handle at either end. Drag the name to move it, drag an end
  to make the line longer or shorter — the name is drawn as large as spans it, so the line *is* the text
  size — and tilt the line to run the name along a coast or a road. Hold **Shift** while dragging an end to
  keep the line straight. There is a box in the strip along the top for the name itself, so you can see
  how it will look as you type it, and *Finish* brings the window back. A name placed this way stops
  following the map's label settings; *Hang it off the marker again* puts it back.
- **Pick a font by looking at it.** The font list no longer disappears behind the edge of the window: it
  opens over everything, flips above the field when there is no room below, and writes every font's name
  in that font — with your place's own name in it, if it has one, so you are choosing the label you will
  actually get. The chosen font is shown under the field as well.
- **Events happen somewhere.** The edit window has a **Place** section of its own, just above
  Characters. *Set place* opens a searchable list of every pin you have made, each one under the place
  it sits inside, so the market in Helim and the market in Waterdeep are never the same line.
- **And it shows you where that is.** Once a place is set you get a crop of its map with the pin on
  it, and the way down to it written out — *Faerun › Helim › Market* — so you can tell at a glance
  which of your three Gates you picked. **Show on map** opens the map window and flies down to it,
  landing close enough to read the streets around it — as close as that particular picture can go
  before it is only a bigger blur, so a hand-drawn town arrives as sharp as a continent does.
- **Your writing suggests the place.** Name somewhere in the description or the content and it is
  offered as a chip under the section: click to take it, ignore it and nothing happens — your own
  choice is never overwritten. Place names are ordinary words, so a mention only counts when the
  sentence means a place: *"born at the market in Helim"*, *"the Helim market"* and *"the market on
  the outskirts of Helim"* all find the right market, while *"the market crashed"* finds nothing.
  Name more than one step of the way down and it follows them: with an inn in every city of a country,
  *"the inn in the Country"* offers you all of them, but *"the inn, in Country's City"* offers the one
  — in either word order, and with as many qualifiers as you care to write. The words that
  earned a chip are marked in the text — underlined, with a coloured dot beside them, so a place
  never looks like one of your characters — and every mention is marked, not just the first.
- **Present, or only mentioned.** Every character on an event now carries a switch: were they *there*,
  or merely *named*? Click it to flip. Which one a character starts as is yours to choose per timeline
  — Timeline Settings → New Items → Attached Characters — and starts out as *Present*. Only the ones
  who were present will be drawn moving across the map later.
- **A birth is an event you can write.** The birth and death events a character makes for itself are
  ordinary events now: give one a place, a description, notes, a tag, the other people who were in the
  room, and saving the character again keeps all of it. Before, every save rebuilt them and quietly
  threw that work away. The character still owns the title, the date and the colour, so renaming
  someone or moving their birthday still moves the event.
- **Show on timeline hides, it no longer deletes.** Untick it and the character's birth and death stop
  being drawn anywhere — canvas, notes panel, calendar — but everything you wrote on them is still
  there, and ticking it again brings it all back. The dates are what decide whether those events exist
  at all: clear a date and its event goes, keep it and the event is yours to fill in whether or not
  anyone can see it.
- **Going in is a journey, not a jump.** Double-click a place with a ring — or press *Enter* on it — and
  the view flies into the patch of ground its map covers while that map grows in to fill the screen, so
  you can see where in the world you just went. The dissolve can be switched off (*Descent fade*, under
  *Map settings* in the *Who is where* window); the flight stays either way, and a scroll or a click
  mid-flight lands you straight away.
- **And coming out is the same journey backwards.** Click a name in the trail along the top and the city
  shrinks back into the spot it occupies on the kingdom, which opens out around it — starting from
  wherever you had got to, not from a view that straightens itself up first. Going somewhere further off
  flies the ends of the trip — out of the city you are standing in, and down into the one you land on —
  so you always see where you left and where you arrived. Longer trips fly faster per leg rather than
  taking forever.
- **Clicking a place in the list flies there too.** An inn three maps down used to simply appear, which
  told you nothing about where in the world it was. Now the view climbs out of wherever you are, crosses,
  and drops into the map the inn is on, the same as walking the trail by hand. And it stops over the
  place you asked for: coming out of an inn to look at the city no longer pulls back to the whole kingdom
  and then hauls you in again on top of the city you just left.
- **Box in what you want to see.** Hold **Shift** and drag a rectangle anywhere on the map, and letting go
  zooms until that rectangle fills the screen. Getting from a continent to one bay is a single gesture
  rather than a dozen scroll-wheel notches, and nothing you drew a box around gets cut off the edge.
- **The flights actually read as flights.** Zooming is judged the way your eye judges it — by how many
  times bigger, not by how many numbers — so going in no longer creeps and then pounces, and coming out
  no longer leaps most of the way in an instant and then dawdles. Both ends move at an even pace the
  whole way.
- **No more waiting on the picture.** A map's image is unpacked before you ask to see it: the maps behind
  the pins you are looking at, and every map along a journey while its first leg is still in the air. Going
  into a big hand-drawn map used to hitch on the frame it started on.
- **One click back out.** Any map that sits inside another has a *Back out to…* button in its top-left
  corner, naming the map above it. No hunting along the trail, and no wondering whether zooming out far
  enough will eventually do it.
- **Move a window out of your way.** Every edit window — a map, a place — can be dragged by its title
  bar. Editing how a pin looks while the pin itself was hidden behind the window was guesswork; now you
  put the window somewhere else and watch the map as you change it.
- **Say how much ground a map covers.** *Edit* a place that opens into a map, then *Set location
  footprint*: a dimmed, dashed box appears around it — that is how much of the bigger map the smaller one
  is — and you drag its corner to make it right. It is drawn only while you are setting it, so a map of
  forty doors is still a map. If both maps have a scale, the box starts out honest — a 5-mile city on a
  50-mile world begins as a tenth of it — and its shape always follows the map behind it, so nothing you
  drag can squash that map out of proportion.
- **How far is that?** Every map has a scale bar in the bottom-left corner, and it tells the truth at
  any zoom: zoom in and it counts down to a mile, zoom out and it counts up to a thousand. Set it by
  typing — *Edit map* draws the bar itself, so *the bar reads 10 miles* means something before you have
  ever noticed there was a bar — or, if you know the distance between two places on your map, hit *Set
  from map*, click the two of them, type how far apart they really are, and the bar relabels itself to
  match. The unit is whatever you write: miles, leagues, days' travel. The bar now sits on a panel of its
  own, so it reads over a pale coastline as well as over dark sea.
- **A ruler for your own map.** The ruler button in the bottom-right corner lets you click any two
  points and read the distance between them in your own units. After the first click the line follows
  your cursor, so you can see what you are measuring before you commit to it — and holding **Shift**
  locks it to straight across or straight down for measuring a road or a border. The line is white inside
  a dark edge and the distance sits on a small plate, both readable on any map, and the crosses and the
  line stay put while you pan and zoom so you can check one leg of a journey against another.
- **Which way is north, and where the compass sits.** *Edit map* → *Place the compass* hands the map
  over: drag the rose where your map has room for it, drag its corner to make it bigger or smaller, and
  turn the rose itself to point at whatever your map calls north — double-click to put north back to
  straight up. All three belong to that map, so an ocean can keep its compass in the corner and a city
  can keep it out of the streets. At any other time the rose turns the *view* instead of the map's north,
  so a compass brushed past can no longer quietly rewrite which way your world faces.
- **Steering without a mouse wheel.** Arrows for the four directions and a + and − for zoom sit under
  the compass, with *Fit* in the middle of them, so a trackpad or a touchscreen can get around the map
  as easily as a wheel can.
- **Turn the map, the way you would on a table.** A dial under the zoom buttons holds the map at any
  angle at all: drag the grip round the ring and the map follows your pointer, with the angle it is held
  at in the middle of the dial. Bring it back near north and it snaps square on its own. Under the dial,
  two curved arrows tip the map fifteen degrees at a time for when you want to count them, and the arrow
  between them flies it back to square. Double-clicking the dial — or the compass rose — straightens it
  too. Everything keeps working while the map is
  tilted: place names and handles stay upright and readable, *Fit* still gets the whole turned map on
  screen with its corners, a shift-dragged box is still the box you drew, and going into a map or coming
  back out keeps your angle through the whole flight. The tilt is how *you* are holding the map, not
  something about the map, so it is never saved — and it is not the same thing as where north is.
- **A lettered grid, so you can tell a reader where.** The grid button under the zoom controls rules the map
  into squares — columns A, B, C… across, rows 1, 2, 3… down — and the place you have selected shows its
  square beside its name, so "the ruins are in D7" is something you can write down. Every square carries its
  own name rather than leaving you to count back to the edge, the names stay upright and the right way round
  however the map is turned, and they step out of the way when you zoom far enough out that they would be
  unreadable. *Edit map* sets how many squares across the map is; the squares are always square, so how many
  rows there are follows from the shape of your picture.
- **Right-click the map for the things you actually do there.** On the map itself: measure from here,
  a new place at this point, copy or save a picture of the map. On a pin: go in, edit it, move it, set its
  label or its footprint, delete it. The picture is copied to the clipboard or saved as a PNG, the same as
  on the relations screen — and if either fails it now says so instead of doing nothing.
- **Closing a half-finished map or place asks first.** *Cancel*, the X, Esc and clicking outside all check
  whether you changed anything, the way every other editor in the app does.
- **Watch it happen.** *Time* in the top bar turns the map into one moment of your story: drag the date
  along the bottom, or press play and let it run at the speed you pick. The places where something is
  happening then keep their colour and wear an amber ring, everywhere else dims, and the count on the
  right says how much of it is on the map in front of you. Pins never move — a town is where it is — so
  what you are watching is the story moving, not the geography.
- **Your characters walk the map.** With *Time* on, everyone who was actually present at a placed event
  appears as a named dot and travels between the places the story put them in. They set out when one
  event ends and arrive when the next begins, because that is all the story says about the road in
  between; before their first placed scene and after their last they are simply not on the map. Someone
  standing in an inn still shows on the city that holds the inn, and on the world map at the kingdom, so
  a journey across three maps is one continuous walk rather than a dot blinking out and back.
- **Show a few, or the whole cast.** *Who is on the map* lists everyone with somewhere to be, and starts
  with all of them ticked — untick down to the two people whose paths you are actually comparing, and
  *All* puts everyone back. *None* clears the lot when it is easier to tick up than to tick down. Two
  characters standing in the same place fan apart instead of hiding each other.
- **Find one person among a hundred and fifty.** There is a search box above the list now, and a picker
  that cuts the cast into sections you can fold away: **by who is moving** puts whoever is on the road
  right now at the top and everyone not yet in the story at the bottom, **by family** gathers each house
  together — everyone joined by blood or marriage, however many steps apart, so a brother's wife's mother
  is in the same group you are — and **by faction** uses what you wrote in each character's Faction
  field. Every section has a tick box of its own, so a whole army or a whole household goes on or off in
  one click.
- **Open the map from someone's own timeline and it is about them.** With a character's timeline on
  screen, the map pin now opens the map on their story: the clock is already running, and the list is cut
  down to the people they were actually somewhere with rather than everyone you have ever written. They
  are drawn as a bigger, white-edged star rather than a dot, so they are the first thing you find. The
  time bar is theirs too: it runs over the same events their timeline shows — the ones they are only
  mentioned in, and their family's births and deaths, included — from the first to the last, and its
  marks, ⏮ ⏭ and play stop only on those. A chip in the cast window says whose circle you are looking at,
  and clicking its X gives you the whole timeline back.
- **Nine flights to cross the world was eight too many.** Going a long way used to fly through every map
  in between, which said "still further" over and over and left you dizzy rather than oriented. Now it
  flies out of the map you are standing on, jumps the middle in one step, and flies into the one you land
  on — the two flights that actually tell you where you left and where you arrived. *Flight*, under *Map
  settings* in the *Who is where* window, changes it: **Ends only** is the new normal, **Every level** is the old behaviour if you
  liked it, and **Arrive** does no flying at all. Short trips are unaffected — there is no middle to leave
  out.
- **And both flight settings are on the map itself.** The flight mode and the dissolve each have a button
  in the corner controls, next to the grid. You work out that nine dissolves are too many while you are
  watching the ninth, not while you are setting the map up.
- **The clock reads dates now, not years.** Someone can be in six places in one year, and a scrubber that
  only knew the year stacked all six on one spot and called it standing still. It reads real dates in
  your own calendar instead — "4 Cinder 1023" — and moves a day at a time, so a war fought over one
  summer plays out properly rather than happening all at once. A story written in whole years still shows
  plain years, exactly as before.
- **Step to the next thing that happens.** The ◀◀ and ▶▶ buttons either side of play go straight to the
  next moment something starts or ends, however far off it is. Nothing between two events is worth
  scrubbing through, and a year that happens to be empty is not worth sitting in. The map *travels* there
  rather than cutting to it: in a fraction of a second everyone walks their road to where they will be, so
  you can see who went where instead of spotting what changed. The **←** and **→** keys do the same, and
  **Space** plays and pauses.
- **And play walks those moments too.** Play used to march a year at a time, which is twenty-five minutes
  of mostly nothing for a three-thousand-year history and over before it starts for one told across a
  fortnight. Now it moves smoothly from one event to the next, so every stop of the run is something
  happening. The speed picker reads as speeds — ¼× to 4× — rather than a number of anything.
- **One line for the clock.** The time bar is a single track now. Every event is a tick along it, taller
  where many happen together, and the handle catches on a tick as you drag near one (hold **Alt** to drag
  past). Click the date to type one in — day, month and year — and go straight there. The track holds
  still as the date changes length, and the handle picks up on the first try instead of showing a
  no-entry cursor. Drag the right end of the bar to make it wider or narrower — it grows from the middle,
  never past the window, and remembers the width.
- **Show a stretch of time, not just an instant.** The ⟷ button puts a second handle on the same track, and
  the map shows everything between the two. Drag either end, or drag the band between them to slide the
  whole window along the story. The step and play buttons step aside while the range is open, since a
  window is something you look at rather than something you play.
- **A stretch of time drawn as a summary, not a tangle.** With the range open, a road walked a hundred
  times is drawn once: one line each way between two places, as thick as the number of *people* who took
  it, with arrowheads for the direction and a ×12 where the same few went back and forth. A road used both
  ways is two lanes side by side. Places where people spent time get a soft disc as big as the time spent
  there, and places where three or more of them met get an amber ring with the count. Hover any road for
  who took it, how many times and when — and the cast window sums up the range in words: how many travelled,
  the busiest road, the longest stay and where they met.
- **Who travelled together, by name.** A group that set out together is labelled on its road by whoever
  leads it and how many went with them — *Mira + 6* — and the cast window lists the biggest groups in the
  range.
- **Only the busy roads, if you like.** *Roads shown* keeps a crowded range readable: pick the busiest 10,
  25 or 50 and those are drawn in full while every other road goes thin and faint — still there, and
  still telling you who took it when you hover it. The cast window says how many were drawn faint.
- **Where people were coming and going.** *Places show* switches the discs from time spent to *arrivals
  vs departures*: each place is as big as the traffic through it, orange where people mostly left and
  blue where they mostly arrived. Hover one for the counts.
- **A storyline chart.** The cast window has a second tab, *Storyline*: one row per place, time running
  left to right, and every person on the map a line that hops from row to row as they travel — people
  who were together run side by side. Hover a line to pick it out, and click anywhere on the chart to
  send the map's clock to that moment. The date the map is showing sits at the top of the window.
- **The map's settings live with the map.** *Movement*, *Trail length*, *Flight* and *Descent fade* moved
  out of Timeline Settings into *Map settings* at the bottom of the *Who is where* window, next to *Roads
  shown* and *Places show*. They are still remembered per timeline, and whatever you had already chosen
  comes with them.
- **One person, in full.** Click a name in the *Who is where* window — or a dot on the map — and the
  window opens a card on them: where they are right now, or which road they are on and when they get
  there, and the event that put them there. It adds up their journeys, the places they went, the time
  spent on the road and how far they travelled in your map's own units — over the range if one is
  open, over their whole story if not. It lists who they were most often with (click one to switch
  to them) and every stop they made, with the ones outside the range faded. Click a stop and the
  map's clock goes there.
- **The two windows point at the same person.** Hover a name in the cast window and that person's dot
  swells on the map; hover a dot and their name lights up in the list. Pick someone out on the map and
  the list scrolls to them. The tick box beside a name still puts them on the map or takes them off — the name
  itself now picks them out.
- **The storyline picks people out too.** With someone picked out, everyone else's line fades and
  theirs is drawn on top. Click a line to pick that person out.
- **Buttons no longer turn blue.** Double-clicking or dragging across a button, a tab, a section title
  like *Map settings* or the storyline used to select its words as text. It does not any more.
- **Give the person card the room you want.** Drag the thin strip under the card in *Who is where* to
  make it taller or shorter. It follows the mouse all the way. Double-click the strip to go back to
  the default. The window remembers the height.
- **Fold away *In this range*.** Click its title to fold it and click again to open it. It stays how
  you left it.
- **A big story plays smoothly again.** With a thousand events and a hundred people, every frame of the
  clock and every range summary took a third of a second. Both are now quick.
- **Fold or open every group at once.** When the *Who is where* list is grouped, *Fold all* at the top
  of the list, above the groups' own arrows, folds all the groups. *Open all* opens them again.
- **The list remembers your search and folds**, for each timeline.
- **Space, ← and → work in the *Who is where* window too**, so you can play and step the story from
  your second screen.
- **Double-click a name to go to them.** The map flies to them and picks them out. If they are not in
  the story at that moment, the clock moves to where they next appear, or where they were last seen.
- **Fold the places list to one level.** Right-click a place in the list and choose *Fold to this
  level*. On a continent, for example, every continent folds and the world stays open, so the whole
  map is easy to get around. Do it again to open them.
- **In the browser, the *Who is where* window**:
  - opens where you left it last time. Chrome and Edge ask once for permission, so that this works
    on a second screen too;
  - closes with the map;
  - when the browser stops it opening by itself, the map tells you so and you can open it with the
    people button, instead of seeing an error.
- **Dragging a range keeps up with your hand.** On a big story the range summary is a lot of drawing, and
  redrawing it on every twitch of the mouse made the handles lag. While you drag, the last summary stays on
  the map, faded, and the new one is drawn the moment you let go.
- **People travelling together are one line.** On the moving trails, four people who meet up and set out
  together are drawn as four thin lines coming in and one thick one going on, rather than four lines on
  top of each other.
- **Families and factions in their own colours.** Group the cast list *by family* or *by faction* and each
  group takes one colour — on the map, on the trails and in the list — so you can watch a house move
  rather than a crowd of individually coloured dots. A fused road carrying two families is two strands
  side by side, one in each colour. *By who is moving* colours the map the same way, so a dot changes
  colour as its person sets off or arrives.
- **Pick a group's colour.** Every group heading in *Who is where* has a pencil. Click it to choose that
  group's colour, and the arrow beside it gives the group its own colour back. The colour is kept for
  the timeline, and a faction keeps it in the relations window's *Chord* view too, the next time you
  open that window.
- **Right-click a character.** On the map or in the list: open their character sheet, show only them,
  hide them from the map or put them back, show everyone again, show their whole journey — the map sets a range from their
  first appearance to their last with only them in it — or jump to their next or previous appearance.
  Hover a character on the map and the dot swells and the cursor changes, so you know there is something
  to click, and a click a few pixels off the dot still reaches them.
- **Menus stay on the screen.** A right-click near the edge of the map or the cast window used to open
  its menu partly out of sight. It moves back in now, whole.
- **The people have a window of their own.** Everything about the cast — *Who is on the map*, the search,
  the grouping and the summary of a range — lives in a separate *Who is where* window now, so the map's
  sidebar is only about places. It opens by itself when you turn *Time* on and closes when you turn it
  off. Drag it onto a second screen and it comes back there next time. Closed it by hand? The people
  button on the time bar brings it back.
- **Four ways to show a journey.** *Movement*, under *Map settings* in the *Who is where* window, offers
  four. **Glide** walks
  each character from one place to the next and leaves nothing behind. **Dashed trail** draws the way they
  came. **Flowing trail** curves each leg and thins it out behind them, so the line looks like a road
  somebody walked rather than a ruler laid between two pins, and puts a ring wherever they stayed put —
  the whole journey at a glance. **Comet** keeps only the last stretch bright and lets the rest fall away
  fast, for watching where everybody is heading right now. Glide and Dashed trail are untouched, so if
  your map already looks the way you want it, it still does.
- **Two people on the same road are two lines.** Journeys curve, and each character curves their own way
  and by their own amount, so half your cast travelling the same route is a braid you can pick apart
  rather than one line drawn on top of itself. It is the same curve every time you look, and unticking
  somebody does not reshuffle everyone else's.
- **You can see where somebody stopped, and for how long.** On the flowing trail, a place where a
  character stayed a while gets a ring, and the longer they were there the bigger it is. Passing through
  on the way somewhere else leaves no ring at all — so a life spent mostly in one city and a life spent
  crossing the world no longer draw the same picture.
- **Ten people regrouping now looks like ten people regrouping.** Characters standing in the same place
  used to fan onto a ring built for two, which is fine for a pair and a heap of overlapping names for a
  war council. The ring grows with the crowd so everybody keeps their elbow room, and three or more in one
  place get an amber ring drawn round the lot of them with the count inside it. You can see an army
  gathering from across the map without reading a single name.
- **Say how much of the way back the trail remembers.** With any of the trails on, *Trail Length* sets how
  far behind a character the line reaches, and it fades out towards the far end so the freshest part is
  the brightest. It is a percentage of your whole story rather than a number of years, so a tale told over
  three days and one told over three thousand both get a tail of the same length on screen. Put it at 0
  for no trail at all, or 100 to draw every step they ever took.
- **An age is not an alarm.** Something that runs for a stretch of the story — a reign, a war, an era —
  used to light its place in the same urgent amber as a battle fought in an afternoon, for the whole of
  its length. Now it gets a ring in its own colour that fills up, clockwise, as the age goes on: full
  when it is nearly over, gone once it has ended. A place inside three ages at once shows three rings,
  and a "+2" if there are more. The amber is saved for something actually happening, so a city inside
  a century-long empire is not on fire for a hundred years.
- **Click someone on the map to pick them out.** Everyone else fades back, and their whole route across
  the map is drawn in their colour: solid where they have been, dotted where they are still to go.
  Click them again, or click an empty part of the map, to bring everyone back.
- **The map tells you what age you are in.** When something long is in force, a small strip above the
  clock names it — up to three at once, with a count if there are more and the full list on hover. It is
  the background the story is happening against: you can watch a march across the map and still see, at
  a glance, that it is happening during the Long Winter.
- **Clear the map off.** A new round button in the bottom-right corner, between the ruler and the ⓘ,
  takes the controls off the map: the pad of arrows and the reminder about scrolling and dragging. The
  compass and the scale bar stay — they are how a map is read. Press it again to bring the rest back, and
  the app remembers which way you left it.
- **The people are not at the bottom of the list.** The Places list now stops at half the sidebar and
  scrolls inside itself, so whoever you are following — and whatever place you have selected — is still
  on screen. With two hundred towns you no longer scroll past every one of them to reach the cast.
- **Nothing at the bottom of the map sits on anything else.** The hint, the clock, the ages in force and
  whatever you are in the middle of editing now stack up from the bottom edge in one column instead of
  all landing in the same spot. Naming a label no longer disappears behind the time scrubber, and the
  cards that ask you something are rounded rectangles rather than pills the size of a hand.
- **A panel for the place you have selected.** It has its own footing at the bottom of the sidebar and
  keeps its full height — the lists above it give way instead — so the place you clicked is readable
  whether or not time is on and however long the cast list is.
- **Names that grow with the map, if you want them to.** A new tick in a map's marker style — *grows with
  the map*. Left off, a place's name stays the same size on screen however far you zoom, like the pin it
  belongs to. Turned on, the name is written on the ground: a label you have stretched across a range of
  hills still spans those hills three zoom steps in, the way it would on a drawn map. Set it per map, or
  per place for the one name that needs it. Draw the label's line after turning it on — the line you drag
  is what fixes its size.
- **Let the map fly with them.** The camera on the time bar hands the view over to the people on the
  map: it goes into the kingdom when they enter it, into the city when they reach it, and back out
  the moment they part ways, always stopping at the closest map that still holds all of them. Turn it off
  and the map stays wherever you put it.
- **Any size map, drawn the same.** The app makes its own display copies of whatever you give it, so a
  40,000-pixel hand-stitched world opens as quickly as a small sketch, and zooming in brings in the
  sharper copy on its own. One exception: a **PNG** bigger than about 9800×9800 has to be opened whole
  and is turned away with a note asking you to re-export it as WebP or JPEG — those two have no size
  limit here at all.
- **Charts of who went where.** Besides *People*, the *Who is where* window now has nine charts, each on
  a tab with its own icon. The button at the right end of the tabs chooses which charts get a tab, so
  the ones you never use stay out of the way, and the window remembers your choice. Only the chart you
  are looking at is worked out, so the others cost nothing. Every chart shows the people on the map,
  over the range if one is open and over the whole story if not:
  - **Storyline**: every place on the map as a row, and each person a line moving between them.
  - **Life strips**: a bar per person across time, coloured by where they were. Hover a stretch for the
    place and the dates.
  - **Busy places**: how many people were at each place, slice by slice of time.
  - **Tables**: for each person, their journeys, the places they went, time on the road, distance, and
    when they were first and last seen. For each place, how many people came, how many visits, the time
    spent there and the most there at once. Click a column to sort by it.
  - **Who met whom**: how often each two were at the same event.
  - **At one place**: choose a place and see how busy it was, and who was there when.
  - **How far apart**: the distance between two people you choose, over time, in your map's own units.
  - **Weave**: each person is a thread. Threads that share a shaded band are people at the same place,
    and the place's name is written where the band starts. Point along a thread to see where that
    person was. How high a band sits means nothing.
  - **Flows**: journeys from place to place, as thick as they were travelled.
- **The charts pick people out too.** Click a name or a line on any chart to pick that person out on
  the map, and hover it to make their dot swell. Over the whole story, click a chart to send the map's
  clock to that moment.
- **Every chart explains itself.** *How to read this*, under each chart, says in a few plain sentences
  what you are looking at, with a small example. Open it once and it stays open for every chart until
  you fold it again.
- **Zoom into a stretch of time.** Drag across any chart that runs over time to zoom in on just that
  stretch, and drag again to go further. *Zoom out*, at the top, or **Escape** goes back one step at a time.
- **One chart leads to the next.** Click a square in *Who met whom* and *How far apart* opens on that
  pair. Click a place, whether a row name, a bar in *Life strips*, a row in the places table or a box in
  *Flows*, and *At one place* opens on it. The tab is added for you if you had hidden it.
- **"They never met."** *How far apart* now says when the two people you picked were never at the same
  event, or when the first of them never met anyone else on the map.
- **Dragging a busy map is smooth.** With hundreds of trails on it, the map used to stutter as you
  dragged it around. It now keeps up with your hand.
- **Every chart says what is under the pointer.** A line above each chart reads out the square, bar,
  band or name you are pointing at, straight away rather than after a tooltip's pause.
- **The charts work like the list.** Double-click a name or a line on any chart, or a row in *Tables*,
  and the map goes to that person. Right-click one for the same menu the list has.
- **An empty chart offers the way out.** When nobody is on the map, or *How far apart* has fewer than
  two people to compare, a *Show everyone* button sits under the message.
- **The chart picker says *Charts*.** It used to be a bare icon at the end of the tabs. It is now
  outlined so it reads as a button, and the tab icons are bigger and easier to see.
- **You can see when someone is hidden.** While anyone is left off the map, a chip above the clock says
  *Showing 3 of 12 · Show all*, and clicking it puts everyone back.
- **Escape backs out.** On the map it closes a menu or the date box, stops a pick or a new place, and
  finishes an on-map edit like moving a place or drawing a label. What you already dragged is kept.
- **Nothing flies away from under an editor.** With *fly with them* on, the map used to follow the cast
  to another map even while you were editing a place there. It now waits until the editor, menu or pick
  is closed. Opening an editor also pauses play. If you turn *fly with them* on when nobody is anywhere
  to fly to, it says so.
- **What happened here shows what is happening now.** In a place's list of events, the ones going on at
  the clock's date are lit in amber. With *Time* on, click any of them to move the clock to it.
- **The clock bar holds still.** The *Time* button no longer shifts the bar while it plays. ⏮ and ⏭
  grey out when there is nothing further that way. The date box opens ready for you to type a year, and
  if the date is outside the story it tells you that it stopped at the story's first or last date.
- **The keys do what you expect.** After you drag a time handle, ← and → step the story rather than
  nudge the handle, and Space plays even while a handle has focus. Space after clicking a button plays,
  rather than pressing the button again. None of these keys reach the map while an editor or menu is
  open. The handles' tooltips mention Alt and PgUp/PgDn.
- **Errors stay until you close them.** An error message now waits for its X instead of fading out,
  and it sits above the clock rather than covering the map.
- **Closing the *Who is where* window keeps it closed.** It no longer reopens by itself while *Time* is
  on. The people button on the time bar brings it back.
- **The pointer no longer covers a road's details.** The card you get by pointing at a road, a stay or a
  meeting on the map used to open under the mouse pointer. It now opens above and to the right of the
  pointer, with space between them. Near the right or top edge of the window it moves to the other side.

## Archive

- **Everything on a timeline, in one list.** A new **Archive** button in the timeline's sidebar
  opens a window that lists every item on the timeline. Pick a type from the chips along the top,
  sort by date, length, name, importance or when you added it, and search titles and descriptions.
  Click a title to jump the timeline to that item; the buttons on each row also open it for
  editing or delete it. The Archive stays open while you do, and closes along with its timeline.
- **A delete in the Archive can be taken back.** Whatever you delete in the Archive, whether items,
  characters, stories, books, chapters, pictures, places, maps, tags or notes, goes to a **Trash**
  tab first. The tab appears once something is in it, and you can restore anything there while the
  window is open. When you close the Archive it lists everything in the trash and asks once before
  deleting it for good, with a warning if a story, book, picture, tag or shared character is used by
  other timelines too. Say no and the window stays open.
- **Your stories, with room to describe them.** The Archive's **Stories** tab lists the stories this
  timeline uses, plus any that nothing uses yet. Click one to edit it beside the list:
  - a summary, status (idea, drafting, done), tense, person, genre, colour and reading order;
  - the story before it and the story after it. Set either one and the other story follows
    suit, so the chain reads the same from both ends;
  - its span, worked out from the first to the last item that cites it;
  - its characters, with the point-of-view ones marked, and its places;
  - the books it belongs to, favourite quotes and notes.

  *Referenced in* folds out every item that cites the story, and you can jump to, edit or delete
  any of them from there.
- **Books and their chapters.** The **Books** tab lists each book's chapters. You can number,
  rename, add and delete them, and see the items that cite each chapter. The tab stays out of the
  way until you have a book; until then a **+ Book** button starts the first one.
- **Stories told in particular chapters.** Under each book a story belongs to, tick the chapters
  it runs through, and the book reads *The Long Winter · ch. 4, 6, 8*. Tick none and the story is
  the whole book. In the **Books** tab each chapter shows its stories; click one to open it.
- **All your pictures in one place.** The Archive's **Media** tab lists the pictures this timeline
  uses, plus any that nothing uses yet, in three groups: images, maps and portraits. Filter by file
  type, sort by date added, usage or size, and search by name. Click a thumbnail to see the picture
  full size. Click a name to rename or describe the picture and see everything that shows it; click
  an item there to jump to it. Deleting a picture that's still in use lists everything that will
  lose it first, other timelines included. Click a group's title to fold it away.
- **Your cast, as a list to look things up in.** The Archive's **Characters** tab lists everyone in
  the timeline with their portrait, birth, death and how many items name them. Search by name,
  nickname, alias or faction, and sort by name, appearances or birth. Click a name to open their own
  timeline. The arrow beside it folds out the items they appear in, who they are to everybody else
  (click a name to go to that person), and the places they have been. The buttons on the row open
  them in the Relations or Characters window, or their family tree.
- **Every place, as the map sees it.** The **Places** tab lists your maps and places the way the Map
  window's list does. Each map sits at the top with its pins under it, and a place that opens into a
  map carries that map's places under it. The arrow on the left folds them away. Click a name to
  open the map there. The arrow beside the name shows what happened there and who was there,
  counting everything inside it, so Faerun includes what happened in Baldur's Gate. Search finds
  maps as well as places. A place or a whole map can be deleted from here, through the same trash
  as everything else. A map takes its own pins with it, and the maps inside it stay.
- **Tidy up your tags.** The **Tags** tab lists the tags this timeline uses, plus any that no item
  carries, with how many items carry each one here and in your other timelines. The arrow folds out
  the items; click one to jump to it. The pencil renames a tag. Rename *dragon* to *dragons* when
  *dragons* already exists and you are asked whether to merge them: every item then carries
  *dragons*, and *dragon* is gone. Tags are shared by every timeline, so a rename or merge happens
  straight away everywhere. A delete goes through the trash like everything else.
- **Your notes, all in one list.** The **Notes** tab lists the notes from the timeline's Notes panel,
  with their dates. Search their text, click a date to jump the timeline there, edit a note where it
  is, or delete it through the trash. The timeline's Notes panel picks up the change without a
  reload.
- **Quotes that look like quotes.** A story's memorable quotes are cards now, in a book face, with
  who said it underneath. *Add quote* opens a small window: type or paste the line, name the
  speaker (your characters are suggested, but anyone can speak), and *Add a line* turns it into an
  exchange between several people. Click a card to edit it.
- **Unsaved changes aren't lost.** Close the Archive, or open another story, book, picture or note,
  while one you are editing has unsaved changes, and you are asked before they are dropped. An item
  you opened for editing from the Archive stays open when the Archive closes.
- **Make a story, book or chapter from the item editor.** Type in the story list and pick
  *+ New story* when nothing matches. Type a book title that doesn't exist yet and pick
  *+ New book*. With a book chosen, *+ New chapter* adds the next one.
- **Renames reach every open window.** Rename or delete a story in the Archive and the timeline and
  the item editor catch up straight away.
- **Characters you took off the timeline are still listed.** When a character's *Show on timeline*
  is off, their birth and death appear in the Archive marked *hidden*. A **Show on timeline**
  button next to each one turns the switch back on and takes you there.
- **Sidebar buttons show what is open.** The Characters, Relations, Map and Archive buttons light up
  while their window is open. Click a lit one to close its window. Tags, Mass add, Reference,
  Export, Settings and Help also light up while their dialog is open.
- **Change many items at once.** Turn on **Edit multiple** in the Archive's **Items** tab and each
  row gets a checkbox. Shift-click to tick a whole run, or use **Select all shown** to tick
  everything the chips and search leave in view (click it again to untick them). A bar then appears
  under the list and changes every ticked item in one go:
  - which zoom levels they show at;
  - their importance and their colour;
  - add a tag to all of them (a new name makes a new tag), or take one off;
  - add them to a story, or take them out of one;
  - move them all to the trash.
- **And many characters, places and pictures.** The **Characters**, **Places** and **Media** tabs
  have **Edit multiple** too, each with its own bar:
  - *Characters:* set their faction, their colour or *Show on timeline* together, or give them all
    the same relation to someone. They can all be adopted by the same parent, or all start
    hating the same person at once. *Replace* ends their other ties of that kind first.
  - *Places:* give the pins one colour, put their markers back to the map's own look, or move them
    under another map. A place keeps its spot on the picture, so pins dropped on Asia by mistake
    land where they were, now on North America, ready to be nudged. Move a whole top-level map
    under a new world map and it gets a door in the middle of that map, named after it.
  - *Media:* put the ticked pictures on one item, or take them off this timeline's items. They stay
    in your library either way.
  - Every tab's bar can also move what is ticked to the trash.
- **Find the loose ends.** The Archive's new **Misc** tab lists what nothing uses and what is
  missing something: pictures nothing shows, tags on no item, stories no item cites, characters
  with no birth, events, periods, ages and notes with no description, and titles used more than
  once. Click a heading to fold its list out, then jump to, open or trash what you find.
- **Bring hidden years back.** *Hidden ranges*, also under **Misc**, lists every stretch of years
  you have hidden from the timeline. *Restore* shows it again, and the timeline follows straight
  away.
- **Your work history, day by day.** *Sessions*, under **Misc**, lists each day you worked on the
  timeline and marks where your last export stopped. Click a day to see what you added, changed or
  removed. *Prune* clears away the days you opened the timeline and changed nothing. *Merge* folds
  neighbouring days into one, so the export's *My work* tab offers fewer, bigger steps.

## Timeline

- **Find an item without opening the Archive.** Press **Ctrl+F**, or the magnifier in the
  timeline's sidebar, and a small search panel floats over the timeline. Type a word and it lists
  every item with that word in its title, description or tags, in date order. **Enter** takes you
  to the next match and **Shift+Enter** to the previous one, or use the arrows beside the box.
  Each time, the timeline pans to the item and makes it pulse. Click a match in the list to go
  straight to it. Drag the panel by its title if it is in the way; **Esc** closes it. It searches
  what the timeline is showing, so an item your filters hide is not found.
- **Errors no longer pass silently.** A few problems used to be written to the log with nothing
  on screen. These now tell you what went wrong:
  - saving a calendar;
  - loading the calendar list;
  - saving timeline settings, where the button used to stay on *Saving…* for good;
  - an item's pictures failing to load;
  - a reference timeline that could not be restored.

- **Dates on the ruler can lean.** Zoomed in far enough, the dates along the timeline sit so close
  together that they run into one another and read as one long string. Turn on *Angled Labels*
  under Tick Labels in the timeline settings and each date leans, so they stay apart and stay
  readable however tight the ticks are. Off unless you turn it on.
- **Crowded events use the empty half of the canvas instead of piling up.** Where a lot of events
  fell close together, the ones at the bottom of the stack ran out of room and were drawn on top of
  each other — sometimes three to the same spot, so only the front title could be read. Now, when
  one side of the line is full, the next event goes to the other side rather than behind its
  neighbour. Events stay on the side you put them whenever there is room for them there. If both
  sides fill up — a great many events at once in a short window — zooming the window out with F10
  gives the timeline more height to work with.
- **Deleted items disappear straight away.** An item deleted from another window no longer
  lingers on an open timeline until you reload it.
- **Ages can be left open-ended too.** "Open start" and "Open end" were meant for ages as well as
  periods, but the boxes never appeared when you edited an age. They do now.
- **An open end can be told to fade out.** A new "Fade out" tick, next to the two above, trails the
  open side off instead of ending it flat: half see-through at the arrow's point, back to full
  colour a year into the age or period. Leave it off and the arrow stays solid, as before.
- **The arrow points at the right year.** Its point now sits exactly on the start or end date
  rather than a little way past it.
- **The bar ends *in* the arrow.** The head is the last stretch of the bar rather than a shape laid
  on top of one, so the bar's own edge no longer shows through a faded arrow, and a rounded age or
  period keeps its round corner on the closed side while the open side runs straight into the
  point.
- **No stray arrowhead left behind.** Turning *Fade out* on or off used to leave the old arrowhead
  stuck to the canvas, floating where it last was.
- **Events no longer draw through an age.** Events stack from the top and bottom edges of the
  window inwards, and in a busy stretch of years they reached the middle and ran straight across
  the age stripe behind them -- the deepest of them coming out on the wrong side of the timeline
  altogether. Every item now stops short of the age band, so an age stays a clean backdrop however
  crowded the years on top of it get. Periods sitting exactly on the line have moved clear of it
  for the same reason.
- **Nor through the periods stacked on it.** The same crowded stretch used to run events straight
  across the period bars underneath them. Events now stop short of the deepest bar on their own
  side of the line, so a period stays readable no matter how many events sit above it.
- **A portrait stays on its own side of the line.** A character's face is several rows tall, and
  a deep one used to hang across the timeline itself — half above the line, half below. It now
  keeps clear by its own height rather than by the height of an ordinary event box.
- **Ages and periods stay behind everything else.** They are drawn first now, so a bar can no
  longer come out on top of an event box that shares its years.
- **A narrow box no longer hides under a wide one.** When the timeline worked out which row an
  item could take, it measured only the width of the item asking — so a small box could be given
  a row a much wider one already reached across. It now measures both.
- **Every zoom level can have its own tick spacing.** Until now one Tick Distance had to do for all
  eight of them, so a thousand years of history was drawn as narrowly as a single day. The calendar
  editor's LOD section has a new **Tick Distance** list -- one box per level, left blank to keep
  using the timeline's own setting. Fill one in and only that level changes, so millennia and
  centuries can breathe while days and weeks pull in close enough to read a season at a glance.
  **Auto** fills in a sensible spread and **Auto LOD** includes it when it resets a calendar.
- **Your existing timelines already have it.** The standard Gregorian calendar has been given that
  spread -- millennia three times as wide, centuries twice, decades a little wider, weeks and days
  half -- so zooming out is worth doing the first time you open this version. A calendar you have
  edited yourself is left exactly as you set it.
- **Pictures in the side panel open full size.** Clicking an item's picture in the data panel
  enlarged the small thumbnail, so it came out blurry. It opens the original now.
- **Removing a picture from an item no longer deletes a map or a portrait.** A map image or a
  character's portrait added to an event from the picture library was deleted when you took it off
  that event, leaving the map blank or the character without a face. It stays now.

## Dates

- **The ruler no longer skips February.** Zoomed in to months, the timeline drew its ticks at even
  twelfths of a year and then looked up what month each one had landed in. A twelfth of 365 days is
  day 30, and February does not begin until day 31 — so no tick ever landed inside it, and the ruler
  read "Jan Jan Mar Apr", one month short with every name after January in the wrong place. Ticks
  are now placed on the days your calendar actually says a month begins, so there is exactly one per
  month and each one is named correctly. The same applies to seasons and weeks, and to a calendar
  with three seasons or sixteen months as much as to the Gregorian one.
- **Zoomed in to days, the ruler shows dates.** It used to count — "Day 348" — which was both hard
  to place and wide enough that the labels ran into each other. It now reads "14 Dec".
- **The year is marked on the ruler at every zoom.** If your calendar's seasons start mid-year, as
  the standard one's do, the season ticks never fell on New Year's Day and the year number had
  nowhere to appear. The turn of the year is now drawn in its own right.
- **The cursor snaps to the ticks you can see.** Now that ticks sit on your calendar's real month
  and season starts rather than on even slices of the year, the cursor was still rounding to the even
  slices — so it came to rest between two ticks, and the further into the year you moved the further
  off it was. Zoomed in to weeks on a 365-day calendar it was a full day out by December. The cursor
  now lands on the tick it is nearest to, whichever day your calendar puts it on, and a new item
  dropped on the canvas lands on that tick and not beside it. Hold Shift to place something between
  ticks, as before.
- **The year number has a line of its own.** Where the turn of the year falls close to a season or a
  week boundary — and in the standard calendar Winter opens thirty days before New Year — the year
  number and the unit's name were printed on the same spot, leaving an unreadable pile of letters.
  The year is now written on a second line below the others, so both can be read.
- **Crowded tick names thin out instead of overlapping.** Zoomed to a level where the names along the
  ruler are wider than the gaps between their ticks, the ones that will not fit are left off. Every
  tick keeps its mark, so nothing moves or disappears — and which names are dropped does not change
  as you pan, so the ruler stays still under the mouse. Turning on *Angled Labels* fits about five
  times as many names in the same space. With the labels angled there is no second line to move the
  year to, so there the year keeps its place and the month or season name beside it is the one left
  off — the year is never the label that goes.
- **A year stands out from the months and weeks around it.** *Show Smaller Non-Year Ticks* used to
  only shorten the sub-year tick marks. It now also prints their names a size smaller and draws the
  year's own mark a pixel heavier, so the turn of the year is easy to pick out of a crowded ruler.
  Turn it off and every tick is drawn the same as before.
- **Angled labels lean the other way.** They now run from upper-left down to lower-right, starting at
  their own tick — the direction a slanted axis is usually drawn.
- **Tick names stay readable over calendar bands.** The names on the ruler are drawn straight onto
  the bands behind them, so a band close to the label's own colour used to swallow it. Each name now
  carries a faint halo in the opposite direction -- dark behind a light label, light behind a dark
  one -- so it reads over any band.
- **Months and seasons now land where their name says.** A date entered at month precision was
  positioned by splitting the year into equal parts rather than by your calendar's actual months, so
  picking February put the item a day early and the timeline labelled it January. Seasons were worse:
  in the standard Gregorian calendar, where Spring starts on the sixtieth day of the year, every
  season came out one off, and Winter — which runs over the new year — could not be chosen at all.
  Both now use the months and seasons your calendar defines.
- **Saving an item no longer nudges its date.** The date fields used to be written back out every
  time you saved, whether or not you had touched them. Change a title and the item could shift by as
  much as a season. An item's position now only moves when you actually change it, and one you
  dropped between two ticks stays exactly where you dropped it.
- **Relation dates open showing the right date.** Opening a character relation to edit it showed the
  first month or season of the year instead of the one it was saved with.
- **Dates millions of years out are read correctly.** Far enough from year zero — past a few
  million years either way — the app could no longer tell which day of the year a date sat on, and
  would read one written on the first of a month as the last day of the month before. The cursor
  and the date editor now hold every day of the year anywhere on the axis.
- **If your seasons look wrong, try Auto DOY.** Seasons are defined by the day of the year they
  start and end on. A calendar whose seasons were filled in with month numbers instead will label
  its ticks wrongly — the **Auto DOY** button in the calendar editor's Seasons section works the
  right days out from your months.

## Reference timelines

- **Reference items no longer hide behind your own.** A timeline drawn underneath now works out
  which rows the real timeline is using and takes the free ones, so its events sit beside yours
  instead of disappearing under them.
- **Ages show through each other.** An age on your timeline that covers an age on the reference
  timeline is now drawn with diagonal slits in the reference age's color — and only across the
  stretch where the two actually overlap, so the slits themselves show you where the one behind
  starts and ends. (Ages sit in the middle of the timeline and cannot move aside the way other
  items can, which is why they get this instead.)
- **Your reference timeline is remembered.** The timeline you picked and the year shift you set
  for it come back the next time you open that timeline, per timeline. If it can no longer be
  loaded — you deleted it, say — it is dropped and the Reference window tells you why.
- **A second set of year numbers, above the ruler.** If your story counts its years from its own
  beginning but you think in real-world dates — or the other way about — the timeline can now show
  both at once. Tick **Second year numbering** in the Reference window (`R`), put a number beside it,
  and the same ticks are numbered again above the axis, shifted by that much: year 0 below, 1450
  above. Untick it and the ruler looks exactly as it did — the number stays where you left it, ready
  for the next time you want the second row. It works whether or not you have a reference timeline
  loaded, and if you have angled ruler labels switched on the upper numbers lean the opposite way, so
  the two rows mirror each other across the axis. It is only a way of reading the ruler — your dates
  are still stored, typed and jumped to in the timeline's own years, and nothing you have saved
  moves.

## Settings

- **The timeline settings panel has been gone over end to end.** It had grown a row at a time and
  it showed — the same kind of thing named three ways, two controls of different types doing the
  same job, and a few rows that did nothing at all. It is now four tabs: **General** (navigation,
  new items, filtering, animation, window), **Canvas** (the preset, the axis, ticks, boxes, bars,
  pictures), **Overlays** (the now line, the hover line, the data range, calendar bands, time
  breaks, measurement) and **Panels** (notes, gallery, calendar, data). Search still looks through
  all four at once.
- **"Show tick labels above everything" takes effect straight away.** It used to need the timeline
  window closed and reopened before the labels actually moved.
- **Animations moved to General.** They happen on the canvas, but they are not a canvas colour —
  they sit with the other settings that change how the app behaves.
- **The built-in presets are called Default (Light) and Dark.** One of them used to be called
  "Default layout settings", which described a database record rather than a look.
- **The calendar bands on the light preset are finally visible.** They were painted white at a few
  percent over a cream canvas, so turning them on appeared to do nothing. They are now the same
  faint ink the rest of that preset uses.
- **Eleven colours got an opacity slider.** Event borders, the data range band, all four calendar
  bands, time break fill and border, two calendar highlights and the data panel's cards all store
  a transparency, and until now there was no way to set it — you got whatever the preset shipped
  with. Nudging one of them no longer quietly turns it solid, either.
- **Opacity sliders are no longer in percent.** They now run 0-255, the range the value is actually
  stored in. In percent the four calendar band sliders all sat jammed against the left end with one
  step left before invisible, because those bands want two to six percent -- and every save nudged
  each colour a hair darker or lighter than you left it. Both are gone.
- **Right-click any slider to type the number in.** A small field opens over the slider itself. Enter
  to accept, Escape to change your mind, and anything past the ends is pulled back to them. Works on
  every slider in the app, not just the opacity ones.
- **The now line and the hover line finally match.** Both pick their style from the same list
  (solid, dashed, dotted) and both have a width — the now line's was fixed at 2 and had no control.
  Tick label style is a list now too, instead of a text box in front of a setting that silently
  ignored anything it did not recognise.
- **Switching presets no longer leaves a colour behind.** Picking a different preset, making a new
  one or resetting a built-in left the split colour controls showing the old preset's colour — and
  a touch of a slider wrote it back over the new one.
- **The calendar panel's own light/dark buttons are gone.** The preset is already the light-or-dark
  choice, and the panel was the only one with a second one inside it. It now follows the preset like
  every other panel.
- **Three settings that did nothing were removed:** Pixels per Subtick, Display Radius and Show
  Guides. Nothing you have saved changes.
- **Every number says what it is measured in**, and the data range's description now says the thing
  that actually matters about it — the notes, gallery and data panels all list the items inside
  that band.
- **Leaving the settings panel asks first.** Nothing in there is written until you press Save, and
  Cancel, the X, Escape and a click outside the panel all used to take a whole panel's worth of
  changes with them without a word. They now ask, the way the item editor does. Untouched, it still
  closes straight away.

## When something goes wrong

- **Failures speak up.** When something behind the scenes fails, you now get a message saying what
  went wrong instead of a button that quietly does nothing. This covers saving, editing and
  deleting notes, timeline and session exports, database import and export, resetting a layout
  preset, and reading your work history.
- A note that fails to save is put back in the box, and one that fails to edit stays open, so you
  do not lose what you typed.
- **Opening a second item while the editor is up no longer throws an error.** It gave you a backend
  error dialog, and behind it an editor already pointed at the new item — so the one you had open
  was gone either way. The editor holds one item at a time, so it now comes forward and asks:
  *Discard and open*, or *Keep editing* and the other item is left alone. Nothing half-typed goes
  without being asked about, and with nothing unsaved it simply switches.
- **Changes you discarded stay discarded.** After closing the item editor with *Cancel* and then
  *Discard*, the very next item you opened was met with the same question again — about the edits
  you had just thrown away. It now opens straight away.
- **A timeline that cannot be opened says why.** It used to open as an empty canvas, which looks
  exactly like a timeline with nothing on it.
- **A filter preset that cannot be read says so** instead of doing nothing when you click it.
- **Messages come in the app's own dialog.** Import and export results, a character that will not open,
  a timeline that cannot load and the rest used to pop up in the browser's plain grey box. They now use
  the same dialog as everything else in the app.
- **App Settings no longer gets stuck on "Working…".** If a backup or a data folder move failed, the
  window went on saying it was working with the button greyed out until you closed it. The reason now
  appears in the settings window itself and the buttons come back, so you can fix it and try again.
- **The two failures that used to pass in silence now speak up as well.** When something behind the
  scenes explained itself, you were told. When it could not — because it never answered at all, or
  because the app had lost touch with its own back end — you were told nothing, which is exactly when
  nothing you do can be saved. Both now say so, once, however many things failed at the same moment.
- **An item that will not load no longer opens as a blank form.** If the editor could not fetch the
  item you asked for, it came up empty and looked like an item you had not filled in yet — so
  retyping and saving made a *new* untitled item in year 0 and left the real one as it was. It now
  says it could not load the item, and will not save until it has.
- **The year calendar says when it is missing something.** If the year's items or the birthdays
  could not be loaded, a line in its toolbar says so, instead of showing a calendar that looks
  empty. A calendar that cannot be read at all says why.

## Speed

- **The window stays responsive during long jobs.** Loading a big timeline, saving, importing or
  moving your media folder no longer freezes the window while it works.
- **Panels fill in faster.** The data panel and the picture gallery now share one lookup per item
  instead of each asking separately, so panning around a picture-heavy stretch does noticeably
  less work.
- **Dragging a busy timeline is smooth again.** Panning used to get dramatically worse the more you
  had written -- a few hundred items and it was a slideshow, a thousand and it barely moved. Two
  pieces of the drawing code were redoing the same work once per item on every single frame. Four
  hundred items now pans about ten times faster and a thousand about twenty, and the cost finally
  grows in step with your timeline instead of running away from it.
- **A timeline window uses about half the memory it did.** The canvas was built out of eight drawing
  surfaces, each the size of the whole window, and it kept all eight whether or not they had anything
  to do -- the ghosted reference timeline, a character's lifeline and the minimised rail were paid for
  even in a window that never showed them. They are now set up when they are needed and given back
  when they are not, which on a full-screen window is something like seventy megabytes returned. No
  change to what you see.

## Help

- **The help covers the new windows.** Press `F1` on the timeline, or **?** → **Help**, and there
  are now sections on characters, on the Relations window and its seven views, and on reference
  timelines — alongside the ones that were already there. `HELP.md` beside the app has the same
  ground in one page.
- **The export panel's size is the size you get.** The pixel size shown under 1× / 2× / 4× used to
  be whatever it was when you opened the panel; it now follows the picture, so changing view or
  moving the year scrubber with the panel open updates it.
- **Tooltips keep out from under the pointer, in every window.** A button's tooltip used to be the
  system's own, drawn right under the arrow where the pointer covered it, and sometimes half off
  the screen. Every window now uses the app's own tooltip. It sits beside the pointer and stays
  on the screen.
