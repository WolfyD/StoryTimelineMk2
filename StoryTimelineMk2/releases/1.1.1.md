## Characters

- **Characters have a window of their own.** The Characters button in the side strip opens a list
  of everyone in the timeline, with a form for each: first and last name, nicknames, aliases,
  race, faction, a state (alive, dead, missing — or anything you type), importance, color,
  description and notes.
- **Faction.** Whatever your world divides itself into — a house, a guild, an army, a cult. It is
  free text, and the box suggests the names you have already used so a cast does not end up split
  between *Night Watch* and *night watch*. The relations views group people by it, and it is
  searchable in the character list.
- **Portraits.** Give a character a picture and their face shows up beside their name in the
  list. Replacing it clears out the old one.
- **Birth and death dates follow your calendar.** They are entered exactly the way item dates
  are, at whatever level of detail you need — a year, a month, a day.
- **Put a character on the timeline.** Tick *Show on timeline* and their birth and death appear
  as events in the character's own color, tagged and attached to them, carrying their portrait
  as a picture. Move a date and the events move with it; untick the box and they go away.

- **Characters name themselves into your items.** Type a character's name — or a nickname, or an
  alias — into an item's description or content and it lights up as you write. When you click
  away they are attached to the item, marked as something the app found rather than something you
  added. Take one off and it stays off.
- **Invent a character without leaving the item.** The character picker in the item editor has a
  *+ New* button that makes one from whatever you typed into the filter box.
- **Every item a character is in, on their own page.** The character form lists their appearances
  with the role you gave them; click one to fly the timeline to it, or the pencil to open it.
- **Birth and death items look like the character.** They draw as a round portrait on the
  timeline instead of a box, they fall back to the character's color when there is no portrait,
  and they can be filtered as *Character*.
- **Edit the character from their birth or death.** Right-click one of those events and pick *Edit
  character*, or open it and use the button next to *Edit item* — either opens the Characters
  window on that character. *Edit item* still edits the event itself.
- **The Characters window opens without the wait.** It is made ready in the background while you
  work, and made ready again each time you close it.
- **Portraits are no longer drowned in color.** The disc behind a portrait is neutral now, with
  the character's color on the ring around it and the stem below — so a picture with a
  transparent background reads as a face instead of a silhouette. Tick *Use highlight color* on a
  character if you liked the filled disc; it now thickens the ring as well, so it shows on a
  portrait that covers the disc completely.
- **Long captions fold onto a second line.** A character's name or a picture's caption that does
  not fit across the image now wraps to two lines instead of losing half of itself.
- **Nothing is drawn through a character's face.** A portrait is several rows tall, and the
  timeline now packs events around all of them instead of only the row the portrait starts in.
- **Highlighted names have room to breathe.** The highlight behind a name you typed now has a
  little padding and a soft border, instead of hugging the letters.
- **A timeline of one character.** *Their timeline* — on the *Appears in* header of the character
  form, or on a right-clicked portrait — opens the timeline again with everything that is not
  about that character left out: their appearances, their birth and death, and nothing else. It
  opens read-only, so it is a view of their life, not another place to edit it.
- **Hand over one character's journey.** Export from inside that window and you get a timeline
  file containing only that character — their events, their pictures, their tags — so you can
  give someone a character without giving them your whole world.
- **A line through their life.** In a character's own timeline, a wave in their color runs along
  the middle of the canvas from their birth to their death, so you can see at a glance how much of
  the world's history they were there for. If you have not recorded one of the two dates, the wave
  is drawn dashed and simply carries on off that edge. Ages are drawn a little lighter in that
  window, so the line stays readable where it passes under them.
- **Who they are to everybody else.** The character form has a *Relations* section: tie a
  character to another as parent, sibling, grandparent, cousin, spouse, in-law, friend, colleague,
  rival — twenty kinds in all, family and social — and the relation shows up on both of their
  pages, read the right way round from each — *parent of* on one, *child of* on the other.
  Relations normally need no dates, since a son is one from birth; tick *From* or *Until* for the
  ones that do, like an adoption or a marriage that ended.
- **Relating somebody is a window now.** *Relate* opens a list of everyone else in the timeline
  with their portrait, years and state, and a search box over it — so you pick a face rather than
  a name from a dropdown. The kind you choose is spelled out underneath both ways round (“Mira is
  daughter of Aldric”), and a button swaps the two if you got them backwards. Every relation the
  character already has is listed beside it; click one to change its kind, its dates or its note,
  or to remove it.
- **“Five characters share this name — family?”** When somebody shares a last name with characters
  you have not related them to, the Relations section offers to sort it out. It opens a list of
  every pair, with a guess at each one from their birth years — sixteen years or more apart reads
  as parent and child, closer than that as siblings. The guesses are only the dates talking, so
  every row can be re-kinded, swapped or unticked, and pairs that are already related say so and
  stay out of it. Nothing is written until you press the button.
- **Mother of, not parent of.** Characters have a *Gender* field — a list of suggestions you can
  type past — and relations use it to pick the natural English word: *mother of*, *son of*,
  *aunt of*, *brother-in-law of*. Where English has no gendered word, as with cousins, or where
  the gender is one it has no word for, the relation reads the neutral way, which is the wording
  working rather than a gap in it. You can set the wording yourself for any kind in the *Kinds*
  editor.
- **Their family is on their timeline.** A character's own timeline now also shows the births and
  deaths of everyone they are related to, so a life reads against the people in it. Relations,
  not surnames — an in-law who shares no name is family, a stranger who happens to share one is
  not. Their relatives draw smaller and a little dimmer than the character whose window it is, so
  it stays clear whose life you are looking at.
- **A character's own timeline is ringed in their color**, all the way round the window, so it is
  never mistaken for the timeline itself.
- **Ages and periods can run off the edge.** *Open start* and *open end* on an age or a period draw
  a fading arrow instead of a hard edge — for the war that was already old when the story begins,
  or the dynasty that outlasts it, without stretching the timeline to a year you do not mean. Tick
  either, or both.
- **The characters window opens straight away.** It was being warmed only halfway, so every open
  paid for loading the page in front of you. It now loads in the background while you work and the
  two things it fetches come at once instead of one after the other.
- **Your own kinds of relation.** *Kinds* next to the *Relate* button opens a small editor: add
  “Sworn enemies” or “Liege / sworn”, say how it reads each way, and it is offered everywhere.
  Removing a kind leaves the relations that used it in place.
- **Picture and portrait captions have their own text size.** Titles like “The birth of …” ran out
  of room under a portrait. *Settings → Pictures & Portraits* now has a size for picture captions
  and a separate one for portrait captions, so you can shrink the one without touching the other.
  Existing timelines keep the size they had.

- **Everyone, all at once.** A new *Relations* button in the side strip opens a window with the
  whole cast as a web: every character a circle with their portrait in it, every relation a line
  in the colour of its kind. Drag someone and they stay where you put them, next time too. Click
  a character and everyone more than one step away fades, so you can see their corner of it.
  Double-click to open them in the Characters window; right-click for their own timeline.
  The web shuffles itself into shape for a second or two when it opens and then holds still, so
  you can read it — however big the cast is.
- **The web as it was in a given year.** Tick *As of year* and drag the slider: relations that had
  not started or had already ended disappear, characters who were not born yet fade almost away,
  and the ones who had died are half-lit. Your world in 1204, and then in 1230.
- **"How are they related?"** Pick two characters and the window spells the chain out — *Risha is
  the mother of Adan, who is the wife of Toma* — and traces it through the web. If nothing
  connects them, it says so.
- **A family tree.** The other half of the same window: the character you chose in the middle,
  their ancestors above, their descendants below, one row per generation, with couples joined by
  a connector their children hang off — so a child with two parents does not tangle the lines.
  Click any relative to re-centre the tree on them. It is built from parents and marriages only,
  since a grandparent on its own does not say who came between.
- **The family tree is a genogram now.** It used to throw away everything that was not a parent or
  a marriage — two cousins who cannot stand each other looked exactly like two cousins who never
  met. Every other tie between two people on the chart is now drawn over it as a curve in its own
  colour, thicker the closer they are, dashed if it is secret or estranged, and jagged if it is
  hostile. Ties reaching right across a big family are drawn faint, so the near ones — the ones a
  tree is read for — stay on top. Men are squares, women circles, anyone else a diamond, with the
  portrait still inside; a cross through the shape means they are dead. And the tree sizes itself
  to the window when it opens instead of arriving at whatever zoom you left the last view at — a
  family too big to show whole at a readable size opens on the character you picked, with the rest
  a drag away.
- **The year and the kinds now reach the family tree.** *As of year* and the *Kinds* checkboxes
  used to disappear when you opened the tree. They are there now: untick *affinity* to read the
  bare family under the curves, or drag the year to see who was already dead in 1204. Unticking a
  kind only takes those curves away — the tree itself is always drawn, or there would be nothing
  left to look at.
- **Everyone on one line, by when they were born.** The new *Arc* view lays the whole cast along a
  single axis in birth order, oldest on the left, with every relation drawn as a curve — family
  arching over the line, everything else looping under it. Click someone and their own ties light
  up while the rest fade back, which is how you follow one person across a cast too wide to take
  in at once. Anyone you never gave a birth year to waits past a dashed fence at the end rather
  than being dropped or quietly invented.
- **The arc can stack into rows by generation.** A checkbox under the spread slider: everyone
  whose parents are not in your cast goes on the top row, their children on the next one down, and
  so on, with birth order still running left to right. It turns the single line into a picture of
  who came from whom without losing the dates — and couples stay on one row even when one of them
  married in from nowhere.
- **A spread slider for the arc.** All the way left and everyone sits the same distance apart:
  neat, and it tells you only who came before whom. All the way right and the spacing is true to
  the years, so a quiet century is as wide on screen as it was in the story and three generations
  crammed into a decade look crammed. Wherever you leave it is where it is next time you open that
  timeline.
- **Factions, in boxes, with the ties that cross between them.** The new *Sociogram* view gives
  every faction a box of its own and stands them in a ring. Relations that stay inside a box fade
  into the background; the ones that cross from one faction to another are drawn full strength, so
  what you see at a glance is where the loyalties run across the lines you drew. Anyone you never
  gave a faction to rings the outside, belonging to nothing — and since they share a box with
  nobody, every one of their relations counts as a crossing. Click a character and their own
  threads brighten without the rest of the picture going flat. If your houses all deal with each
  other the middle of the ring fills in solid; the **Crossing ties** slider turns the crossings
  down until you can see the boxes again, and remembers where you left it.
- **The *Knots* view actually shows the knots.** The groups were there in the maths, but they
  settled on top of one another, so you could not see them. Now each group of people who mostly
  know each other shoves the others away and sits under a soft coloured blob of its own, labelled
  with whoever in it has the most ties — *Bran Grimsby and 25 others*. The whole picture is sized
  to fit the window when it settles, so nothing starts off the bottom edge. A **Knot distance**
  slider in the sidebar decides how hard the groups push each other away — slide it right for
  clear daylight between them, at the cost of a smaller picture, since the view zooms out to keep
  everything on screen. It reaches twice as far right as it first did, which is what a big cast
  needs, and where you leave it is where it is next time you open that timeline. The knots
  themselves stay as tight as they were wherever you put the slider — it is the threads running
  *between* the groups that give, so pushing the groups apart no longer pulls them to bits.
- **Follow the route across the grid.** When the finder has worked out how two people are
  connected, tick *Show the route on the grid* in the *Matrix* view and it draws the answer:
  every step of the chain is ringed on the square where those two people meet and numbered in
  order, with a dotted line running from one step to the next through the square of whoever they
  have in common. Handy on a big cast, where reading the sentence and then hunting for the squares
  is the slow way round.
- **And along the family chart.** The same tick box is there in the *Genogram* view, where it
  numbers everyone on the route in order and lights the chart's own lines between them — down into
  a marriage, along a row of brothers and sisters — so you can see the answer as a path through
  the family rather than read it as a sentence. It only offers this when both ends are on the
  chart in front of you: a genogram is one family around one root, not the whole cast, and a
  trail that jumped over people who are not drawn would be worse than no trail.
- **A right-click on the family chart stops moving it.** Right-clicking somebody in the *Genogram*
  used to re-centre the whole chart on them at the same moment it opened their menu, sliding the
  person you were aiming at out from under the cursor. Now it rings them and opens the menu, and
  nothing moves; a left click still re-centres, as it always did.
- **Which houses actually deal with each other.** The new *Chord* view stands your factions round
  a circle and runs a ribbon between every pair that has ties, as thick as the number of them. A
  faction's slice is as wide as its dealings, not its size, so a house of forty who keep to
  themselves takes up less of the circle than a house of five everybody needs something from — and
  a ribbon that loops back onto its own slice is a faction that mostly talks to itself. Click any
  slice or ribbon and it lights up while the rest fades, with the list of exactly who it is made
  of down the side; click it again to see the whole picture back. There is a second grouping too:
  switch to *Kinds* and the circle is your relation categories instead, showing how many people
  have family ties and rivalries both.
- **Who nobody knows.** A corner of the sidebar lists the characters no relation mentions at all,
  which is usually the ones you meant to get back to.
- **The Relations sidebar and the Characters list can be dragged wider.** Grab the edge beside
  either one and pull: long names stop being cut off, and the width is remembered.
- **Find a face.** A search box over the whole cast pulls whoever you type to the middle of the
  window.
- **A character can belong to every timeline.** Tick *Shared character* and they show up in every
  timeline's cast, while still counting as one person — the same portrait, the same dates, the
  same relations, edited in one place. They keep the timeline they were made in as their home.
- **Say how close two people are.** Every relation now carries a closeness from 0 to 100, and the
  line between them in the Relations window is drawn thicker the closer they are — and pulls them
  harder together when the web lays itself out. New relations start in the middle.
- **Half-sisters, step-fathers, cousins once removed.** A relation can take a degree, which reads
  into its wording everywhere the relation is named: *half-sister of*, *cousin once removed of*.
- **Estranged, secret, adoptive, former, alleged.** A relation can also carry a state word. It
  leads the wording — *estranged half-sister of* — and changes the line: a secret or alleged tie
  is drawn as long dashes, an estranged or former one as short ones.
- **Character and relation dates are as fine as item dates.** Birth, death and the years a
  relation held are now stored the way items are, so a date picked to the day stays on the day
  instead of rounding to its year. Existing characters and relations were converted in place.
- **Matrix.** The whole cast down the side and across the top, with a square wherever two of them
  are related — brighter the closer they are. People who share a faction sit together, the biggest
  house first and everyone who belongs to nothing at the end, so the blocks fall along the
  diagonal; the character you have selected gets their row and column lit up. Click a square and
  the two people it crosses drop into the *How are they related?* boxes, which then spells their
  connection out and traces it into every other view — and the square you picked is outlined,
  with its row and column lit, so you can see what you just asked about. Click a name down the
  side to set the first of the pair, one across the top to set the second.
- **The chain between two people, with what hangs off it.** The new *Chain* view takes the two
  characters in *How are they related?* and lays the route between them out left to right, with
  each step written over its line — *mother of*, *husband of*, *rival of*. Round everybody on the
  way is a small circle for each of the other people they are directly related to, marked with
  their initials: up to five of them, with a *+3* where there are more. So you see not only how
  two characters connect, but who else is standing next to the connection. Drag anyone to tidy the
  picture up — the side circles follow the person they belong to — and untick *Side circles* for
  the bare route.
- **Right-click means the app now.** Anywhere in the Relations window you get its own menu instead
  of the browser's: on a character, *Their timeline*, *Open in characters*, *Centre the genogram
  here*, and — in every view — the two ends of *How are they related?*, so you can pin down
  somebody you have just spotted and might not find again. On the background, *Fit to window* and
  *Unpin all*. Copy and Save moved to two small buttons in the top-right corner of the picture.
- **You do not lose who you clicked.** The selected character is ringed in white in every view, and
  if a re-drawn chart would leave them off the edge the window brings them back into the middle —
  only when they have actually gone out of sight, so a chart that already shows them holds still.
- **The genogram opens as a family tree.** Its three *Kinds* boxes now start unticked, so you get
  the bare tree and add the friendships, rivalries and affinities over it when you want them,
  rather than clearing a thicket before you can read anything.

## Timeline

- **Deleted items disappear straight away.** An item deleted from another window no longer
  lingers on an open timeline until you reload it.
- **Ages can be left open-ended too.** "Open start" and "Open end" were meant for ages as well as
  periods, but the boxes never appeared when you edited an age. They do now.
- **An open end can be told to fade out.** A new "Fade out" tick, next to the two above, trails the
  open side off instead of ending it flat: half see-through at the arrow's point, back to full
  colour a year into the age or period. Leave it off and the arrow stays solid, as before.
- **The arrow points at the right year.** Its point now sits exactly on the start or end date
  rather than a little way past it.
- **The bar ends *in* the arrow.** The head is the last stretch of the bar rather than a shape laid
  on top of one, so the bar's own edge no longer shows through a faded arrow, and a rounded age or
  period keeps its round corner on the closed side while the open side runs straight into the
  point.
- **No stray arrowhead left behind.** Turning *Fade out* on or off used to leave the old arrowhead
  stuck to the canvas, floating where it last was.

## Reference timelines

- **Reference items no longer hide behind your own.** A timeline drawn underneath now works out
  which rows the real timeline is using and takes the free ones, so its events sit beside yours
  instead of disappearing under them.
- **Ages show through each other.** An age on your timeline that covers an age on the reference
  timeline is now drawn with diagonal slits in the reference age's color — and only across the
  stretch where the two actually overlap, so the slits themselves show you where the one behind
  starts and ends. (Ages sit in the middle of the timeline and cannot move aside the way other
  items can, which is why they get this instead.)
- **Your reference timeline is remembered.** The timeline you picked and the year shift you set
  for it come back the next time you open that timeline, per timeline. If it can no longer be
  loaded — you deleted it, say — it is dropped and the Reference window tells you why.

## When something goes wrong

- **Failures speak up.** When something behind the scenes fails, you now get a message saying what
  went wrong instead of a button that quietly does nothing. This covers saving, editing and
  deleting notes, timeline and session exports, database import and export, resetting a layout
  preset, and reading your work history.
- A note that fails to save is put back in the box, and one that fails to edit stays open, so you
  do not lose what you typed.

## Speed

- **The window stays responsive during long jobs.** Loading a big timeline, saving, importing or
  moving your media folder no longer freezes the window while it works.
- **Panels fill in faster.** The data panel and the picture gallery now share one lookup per item
  instead of each asking separately, so panning around a picture-heavy stretch does noticeably
  less work.
